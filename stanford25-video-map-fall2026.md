# Clinical Correlations: the Stanford Medicine 25 map, Fall 2026

BIO 005 Human Physiology, Yuba College. Sep 2 2026.

**"Clinical Correlation" is the official name of this recurring element**, on week pages, note sheets, and lab worksheets, these items appear under that label. Student-facing definition, one line: *A Clinical Correlation shows you a real bedside exam, then asks you to explain the physiology that makes it work.* The name is deliberate: it is the same term students will meet in nursing and medical curricula, so the course vocabulary is career vocabulary.

Decision: bedside exam videos from Stanford Medicine 25 are woven through the course as **observation-based Clinical Correlation questions** on note sheets and lab worksheets. The pattern every time: watch the maneuver  ->  name the physiological variable being interrogated  ->  predict what an abnormal finding would mean  ->  explain the mechanism. The clinician's hands are the problem statement; the physiology is the answer.

Why this works as assessment: these questions have no answer key anywhere, because they are written about moments in a video. They are not AI-proof, nothing unproctored is, but copying the answer requires finding the moment, capturing it, describing it, and translating an answer back, which is more work than watching and thinking. The verified layer remains the midterm teach-backs.

**Rules of use**
- Link to the Stanford page, never re-host or download the video. The content is ©Stanford University, free to view is not freely licensed. Linking implicates no copyright; copying does. Their pages are free and meant for this.
- **Always assign the page, not the bare video.** Every Stanford 25 exam page pairs its video with a full written step-by-step guide. The written guide is the accurate text equivalent that satisfies 508/ADA equivalent access, since the YouTube captions may be auto-generated and unverified for medical vocabulary.
- **Accessibility rule for every Clinical Correlation question: it must be answerable from the video OR the written guide alone.** All nineteen current questions pass. Any future question that depends on seeing something the text guide doesn't describe gets rewritten or cut.
- Caption spot-check, once per assigned video, ten seconds each: open the video, click CC, read the track name. A track labeled "English" is human-provided and fine to rely on; "English (auto-generated)" alone means the written guide is carrying the accessibility load, which the page-not-video rule already handles. Log results in the compliance notes.
- Anchor questions to named maneuvers ("when she performs the Rinne test..."), not timestamps. Maneuver-anchored questions survive video edits and work identically from the written guide. Add timestamps only after previewing a video yourself, if you want them.
- Every observation question ends in a *why*. The observable fact is the entry ticket, never the answer.
- These are Tier 1 material (note sheets, lab worksheets). Midterm prompts ask students to *teach the test*, what it interrogates, why it works, what a given abnormal result localizes.

## Week 4, The action potential

**Carpal Tunnel Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/carpaltunnel.html
A conduction problem in a living human: compression of the median nerve. Serves comps 53, 60, 61, 63.
1. Tinel's and Phalen's maneuvers both provoke tingling. What are these maneuvers doing to the nerve, and why does a compressed axon fire when a healthy one stays quiet?
2. Sensory symptoms appear before motor weakness in carpal tunnel. Using what you know about axon diameter and myelination, propose a reason, then explain what that predicts about conduction velocity testing.

## Week 5, The nervous system (the anchor week)

**Deep Tendon Reflexes**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/tendon.html
The stretch reflex, graded and compared. Serves comps 73, 74, 75, 79, 121.
1. The examiner strikes the tendon, not the muscle. Trace what the hammer actually stretches and every neuron the signal crosses before the muscle twitches. How many synapses?
2. Reflexes are graded 0 to 4+. Name one lesion site that would produce 0 and one that would produce 4+, and explain both directions using upper vs lower motor neuron logic.
3. Why does the examiner ask the patient to clench their teeth or hook their fingers (the Jendrassik maneuver) when a reflex seems absent? What does that reveal about descending control of the reflex arc?

**Pupillary Responses**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/pupillary.html
Afferent vs efferent detective work; autonomic dual innervation live. Serves comps 108, 123, 125.
1. Light in one eye constricts both pupils. What does the consensual response tell you about where the afferent signal goes?
2. In the swinging flashlight test, one pupil appears to dilate when the light swings to it. Is the broken link afferent or efferent? Defend your answer.
3. Which division of the autonomic nervous system constricts the pupil, which dilates it, and what does pupil size at rest tell you about autonomic tone?

**Fundoscopic Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/fundoscopic.html
The only place the CNS and its vessels are visible from outside. Serves comps 78, 110.
1. The optic disc the examiner finds is the blind spot. Why is there no vision at exactly that point?
2. Papilledema (a swollen disc) signals raised intracranial pressure. Trace why pressure inside the skull shows up in the eye.

**Cerebellar Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/cerebellar.html
Serves comp 120.
1. Finger-to-nose and rapid alternating movements test the cerebellum, yet a cerebellar patient has normal strength. What exactly is the cerebellum contributing, if not force?
2. Predict which side of the body shows the deficit relative to a one-sided cerebellar lesion, and contrast that with the corticospinal pattern.

**Gait Abnormalities**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/gait.html
Serves comps 118, 120, 121.
1. Pick two of the abnormal gaits demonstrated. For each, name the level of the motor hierarchy that has failed and the physiological job that level normally does.

**Internal Capsule Stroke**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/ics.html
One small lesion, a whole-side deficit. Serves comps 77, 119, 121.
1. Why does such a small lesion produce weakness of the entire opposite side? What anatomical fact about the corticospinal pathway makes the internal capsule a bottleneck?
2. The face, arm, and leg are all weak, but sensation may survive. What does that dissociation tell you about where motor and sensory pathways run?

*Enrichment shelf for this week:* Parkinson's Exam, Involuntary Movements & Tremor, basal ganglia physiology for students who want more.

## Week 7, Hormones

**Thyroid Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/thyroid.html
Serves comp 138.

## Week 9, The heart as a pump

**Cardiac Second Sounds**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/cardiac.html
Serves comps 165, 167.
1. S2 is two valves closing. Which two, what pressure reversal closes each, and why does inspiration split them apart?
2. Predict what a loud P2 says about pressure in the pulmonary circuit, and trace the mechanism.

**Precordial Movements**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/precordial.html
Serves comps 165, 166.
1. The apex beat the examiner palpates happens during which phase of the cardiac cycle? What is the ventricle doing at that instant?
2. A sustained, displaced apex beat suggests a chronically loaded ventricle. Connect that finding to end-diastolic volume and wall stress.

**Diastolic Murmurs Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/DiastolicMurmursExam.html
Serves comp 167.
1. A murmur between S2 and S1 occurs during diastole. Name the two valve failures that make noise then, and explain the pressure gradient driving each sound.

## Week 10, Pressure, flow, and blood pressure

**Pulsus Paradoxus and Blood Pressure Measurement**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/bppp.html
The definitive technique video for comp 182; serves 176.
1. What are the Korotkoff sounds physically, and why is there silence above systolic and below diastolic pressure?
2. Normal inspiration drops systolic pressure a few mmHg. Trace why, using venous return and ventricular interdependence, then explain what an exaggerated drop (the pulsus) reveals.

**Neck Veins & Wave Forms (JVP)**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/neck-exam-jugular-venous-pressure-measurement.html
Preload made visible. Serves comps 170, 181.
1. Why does the examiner use the jugular vein as a manometer for the right atrium? What has to be true of the venous column for that to work?
2. Predict the JVP in a patient with volume overload and in one with hemorrhage, and connect each to end-diastolic volume via Frank-Starling.

**Ankle Brachial Index**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/ankle-brachial-index.html
Pressure gradients and resistance in one number. Serves comp 174.
1. The ABI divides ankle pressure by arm pressure. Why is a value below 0.9 evidence of increased resistance between the two cuffs? Use the flow equation.

**Ascites & Venous Patterns**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/avp.html
Starling forces gone wrong, at the bedside. Serves comps 178, 179.
1. The examiner demonstrates shifting dullness. Which Starling force has changed in portal hypertension, and which has changed when ascites comes from low albumin? Two different mechanisms, same fluid.

**Aortic Regurgitation Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/aorticregurgitation.html
1. AR produces a wide pulse pressure. Explain both halves: why systolic rises and why diastolic collapses.

## Week 11, Blood and defense

**Lymph Node Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/lymph.html
Adaptive immunity's geography. Serves comps 253, 255.
1. Why do lymph nodes enlarge near an infection? What is physically happening inside a reactive node?
2. The examiner distinguishes tender-mobile nodes from hard-fixed ones. Connect each pattern to what the immune system is doing.

**Tongue Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/tongue.html
Serves comps 148, 149.
1. A smooth, sore tongue appears in B12 and iron deficiency. Connect this to what rapidly dividing cells need from erythropoiesis's ingredient list.

**Spleen Exam**, https://stanfordmedicine25.stanford.edu/the25/spleen.html
Serves comp 150.
1. Why is the spleen the organ that enlarges when red cell destruction accelerates? What is it doing to red cells all day?

## Week 15, Integration

No new videos. The patient-file case conference asks the reverse question: given your patient, *which* bedside exams would you perform, what variable does each interrogate, and what do you predict each will show? Students assemble their own exam from everything above, which is the course's thesis stated backward.

## Weeks without a Stanford 25 fit

Weeks 1 to 3 (foundations, chemistry, membranes), 6 (muscle, strength testing appears inside the reflex and stroke videos), 8 (reproduction), and 14 (renal, though the JVP video returns naturally for volume status). No forcing; the videos go where the physiology is.

## Excluded by decision

Per Dr. Rennie, Sep 2 2026: the **pelvic exam, rectal exam, and breast exam** are excluded from the course and must not be assigned or linked, now or in future weeks, including Week 8 (reproduction), which is the one place a future build might reach for them. Also outside scope by fit rather than decision: dermatology, newborn exams, joint/musculoskeletal exams, and ultrasound.

## The cranial nerve exam itself

Stanford 25 covers the pieces (pupils, fundoscopy, gait) but has no single start-to-finish cranial nerve exam video, that one is worth recording yourself during the recording block: ten minutes, a willing family member, narrated. Until then, Week 5 runs on the pieces above, which cover the same pathways.
