How to read this packet

For students: this is a checklist of everything this course asks you to be able to do, and what it assumes you arrive with. Work down it. When you can do the thing without looking, that one is finished.

For evaluating institutions: the full assessed content of BIO 005, organized by midterm and week, each competency tagged for written lecture examination, laboratory work, or both, preceded by the anatomy, chemistry and quantitative skills assumed on entry.

These 268 competencies, each one a single thing you should be able to do, are the whole of what BIO 005 asks. They are what the Mastery Physio OS tracks and what every exam is written from. Nothing is assessed that is not on this list.

Competencies are grouped by midterm, then by week in teaching order, then by topic. LECTURE means it is on that midterm. LAB means you produce it on real output: a trace, a curve, a set of values or a calculation. Many carry both, because explaining a mechanism and reading what it does are two different skills.

This Fall 2026 edition renumbers the list to follow the teaching order and the three-midterm structure. If you saw an older packet with five units, this one replaces it.

What this course assumes you already have

Twenty-seven entry expectations, in three areas. They are not part of the 268 competencies and no exam question asks them on their own. They are listed because physiology is built on them, and because a student who knows which of them is shaky can fix it before the week that needs it arrives. Each one is tagged with the midterm block that leans on it.

Anatomy assumed on entry

  • The cell and its parts. Plasma membrane, nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum. What each one is for. MIDTERM 1
  • The four tissue types. Epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous. Enough to tell them apart and say what each does. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • The neuron. Cell body, dendrites, axon, axon terminal, myelin. Which end receives and which end sends. MIDTERMS 1 & 2
  • How skeletal muscle is built. Muscle, fiber, myofibril, sarcomere. Nested, biggest to smallest. MIDTERMS 1 & 2
  • The heart. Four chambers, four valves, and the path blood takes through them in order. MIDTERMS 2 & 3
  • Blood vessel types. Artery, arteriole, capillary, venule, vein, and which direction each carries blood. MIDTERMS 2 & 3
  • The respiratory tract. Trachea down to alveolus, in order. What an alveolus is next to. MIDTERMS 2 & 3
  • The nephron. Glomerulus, capsule, proximal tubule, loop, distal tubule, collecting duct. In order. MIDTERM 3
  • The endocrine glands. Where the pituitary, thyroid, adrenals and pancreas are. Not their hormones, just where they are. MIDTERM 2
  • The digestive tract. Mouth to anus in order, plus liver, gallbladder and pancreas hanging off it. MIDTERM 3

Chemistry assumed on entry

  • Ions and charge. Sodium, potassium, calcium and chloride carry charge, and that charge is what makes a nerve fire and a muscle contract. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Concentration and gradients. What a concentration is, and what it means for something to be more concentrated on one side of a membrane than the other. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Water, polarity and solubility. Why water dissolves salt but not fat, and what hydrophilic and hydrophobic mean. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Acids, bases and pH. What pH measures, which direction is more acidic, and roughly what blood pH is. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Buffers. What a buffer does when acid is added, in one sentence. MIDTERMS 1 & 3
  • Proteins and shape. That a protein does its job because of its shape, and that heat or a pH change can wreck that shape. MIDTERMS 1 & 2
  • ATP and energy. That ATP is the cell's energy currency and that splitting it releases energy the cell can use. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Units and simple calculation. Reading and converting the units this course uses: molar and millimolar, mmHg, liters per minute, percent. ALL THREE MIDTERMS

Quantitative skills assumed on entry

  • Metric prefixes and unit conversion. Milli, micro and kilo, and moving between them without looking it up. Multiply molar by 1,000 to get millimolar. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Counting particles, not molecules. Osmolarity counts particles. A salt that splits into two ions contributes twice what a sugar that does not split contributes. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Proportions and percent change. If one number doubles and another halves, what happens to their product. Percent of a starting value, not of the final one. MIDTERMS 2 & 3
  • Rates, and what per means. A rate is an amount per unit time, and it needs both parts. 5 liters is not a rate. 5 liters per minute is. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • Reading a graph. Which variable goes on which axis, reading a value off a curve, and knowing that a steep part of a curve means a small change in x makes a big change in y. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • What a log scale does. You do not need to calculate logs. You need to know that one pH unit is a tenfold change, so 7.0 to 6.0 is ten times more acidic, not a little more. ALL THREE MIDTERMS
  • In minus out. If more comes in than goes out the amount rises, and by how much. This is arithmetic, and it is the most used tool in the first weeks. MIDTERMS 1 & 3
  • Charge, and why mEq is not mM. Milliequivalents count charge. For a singly charged ion the two numbers are the same. For calcium, which carries two charges, they are not. MIDTERMS 1 & 2
  • Sanity checking an answer. Knowing roughly what a normal value is, so an answer that is a hundred times off looks wrong to you before you hand it in. ALL THREE MIDTERMS

Week 1 · How physiology works and what keeps you steady

Foundations of Physiology

  1. Physiology and levels of function. Define physiology and place a given process at the correct level of organization from molecule to organism, then state the level at which that process is best explained. LECTURE
  2. Structure and function relationship. Predict how a change in the structure of a molecule, cell, tissue, or organ alters its function, using examples from two different organ systems. LECTURE LAB
  3. Homeostasis defined. Define homeostasis, regulated variable, and setpoint, and distinguish homeostasis from chemical equilibrium and from steady state. LECTURE
  4. Feedback loop components. Diagram a negative feedback loop labeling stimulus, sensor, afferent path, integrating center, efferent path, effector, and response, and trace body temperature or blood glucose through every step. LECTURE LAB
  5. Negative and positive feedback. Distinguish negative from positive feedback by the direction of the response and give a physiological example of each, explaining why a positive feedback loop needs an outside event to end it. LECTURE
  6. Feedforward control and acclimatization. Explain anticipatory feedforward control and acclimatization and identify which one is operating in a given scenario. LECTURE
  7. Local and reflex control pathways. Distinguish a local control pathway from a long distance reflex pathway and classify a given response as one or the other. LECTURE
  8. Mass balance. Apply the mass balance equation to a solute or to body water and calculate the intake, production, and output combination that holds the amount in the body constant. LECTURE LAB

Quantitative Skills for Physiology

  1. Units and unit conversion. Convert among the units used in physiology including molarity, osmolarity, milliequivalents, mmHg, liters per minute, and percent solutions. LECTURE LAB
  2. Graphing and data interpretation. Construct a labeled graph with the independent variable on the x axis, and read slope, direction, and trend from a physiological data set. LAB
  3. Experimental design and controls. Identify the hypothesis, independent variable, dependent variable, and control condition in a physiology experiment and state what the control rules out. LAB
  4. Measurement error and variability. Distinguish random from systematic error and explain why physiological measurements are repeated and averaged. LAB
