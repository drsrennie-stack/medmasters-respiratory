1Reaction time, the ruler drop How long it takes a signal to go eye, brain, hand. Needs a 12 inch ruler and one other person.
  1. Rest your forearm on a table with your hand off the edge, thumb and finger about an inch apart.
  2. Your partner holds the ruler so the zero end sits between your fingers. You watch the ruler, not their hand.
  3. They drop it without warning. You catch it. Write down the centimetre mark where your fingers closed.
  4. Five drops with your dominant hand, then five with the other. Then five more with your dominant hand while you count backwards out loud from 100 by threes.

Do not let your partner count down or give any signal. The whole point is that you cannot predict it.

2Two point discrimination, the paperclip test How close two touches can get before your skin stops telling them apart. Needs a bent paperclip and the mm scale on page 3.
  1. Bend a paperclip into a U with two straight ends. Set the ends 20 mm apart against the scale on page 3.
  2. Close your eyes. Your partner touches the skin lightly with either both ends or one end, mixing them up, and you say "one" or "two" each time.
  3. Narrow the gap by 2 mm and go again. Your threshold is the smallest gap where you still get it right.
  4. Test all five sites. Press only hard enough to feel it, the same amount every time.

Include the one-point touches. Without them you will just say "two" every time and get a meaningless number.

3Standing up, and what your heart does about it Autonomic control caught in the act. Needs a clock with a second hand or your phone timer.
  1. Lie down flat, or sit fully reclined, for five quiet minutes. Do not skip this part.
  2. Count your pulse for a full 30 seconds and double it. That is your baseline.
  3. Stand up. Start counting immediately and take a 30 second count in each window below.
  4. Do the whole thing next to a wall or a bed.

If you feel lightheaded at any point, sit or lie down straight away and stop the test. Write down that it happened, that is real data, and finish the rest of the packet without it.

4Vision, using the charts on page 3 Three quick things. Keep your usual glasses or contacts on, and say so in the table.
  1. Blind spot. Hold page 3 at arm's length. Cover your left eye, look straight at the cross, and bring the page slowly toward your face. The dot disappears, then comes back. Measure your distance from the page the moment it vanishes. Swap eyes and watch the cross go instead.
  2. The fan. One eye at a time, from about 16 inches, looking at the center. Do all the spokes look equally dark, or are some directions softer?
  3. Near text. Page 3 at 16 inches, one eye at a time. Smallest line you can read cleanly.

This shows you how the eye is built. It is not an eye exam, and a printed page cannot measure your vision. If a spoke really does look different, take that to an optometrist.

5Hearing range, and standing still with your eyes shut Needs any free tone generator on your phone, and a wall to stand next to.
  1. Set the volume low first, comfortable at 1000 Hz, then do not touch it again.
  2. Climb from 1000 Hz: 4000, 8000, 12000, 14000, 16000, 18000. Write down the highest you can honestly still hear. Then go down: 200, 100, 60, 30 Hz, and write down the lowest.
  3. If someone older or younger is around, run the high end on them and note both ages.
  4. Balance. Feet together, arms at your sides, next to a wall. Time how long you stay steady with your eyes open, then closed. Stop at 60 seconds either way.

Phone speakers and earbuds are not calibrated, so this shows the shape of your hearing range, not your hearing level. Keep the volume low and stop if a tone is uncomfortable. Stay within arm's reach of the wall for the balance test.
